How to Choose an Accredited Testing Laboratory (ISO/IEC 17025 Explained)

A practical checklist for selecting a testing lab: accreditation scope, methods, turnaround, sampling, pricing and red flags.

By the Lab.Works Editorial Team · Updated September 2026 · Editorial policy

Choosing the wrong lab costs more than the invoice: rejected certificates, re-sampling, missed launches and — in regulated industries — compliance failures. This checklist is what experienced QA managers use.

1. Check accreditation — and the scope

ISO/IEC 17025 is the international standard for testing and calibration laboratories. It certifies that a lab has a working quality system and is technically competent for specific methods. The key word is specific: accreditation applies only to methods listed on the lab's scope of accreditation. Always download the scope from the accreditation body (A2LA, ANAB, UKAS, CALA, NABL, DAkkS, etc.) and confirm your test method and matrix are listed.

Clinical labs have their own frameworks: CLIA and CAP in the US and ISO 15189 internationally. Pharmaceutical QC labs work under GMP, and non-clinical safety studies under GLP.

2. Confirm the exact method and detection limits

Ask for the method reference (EPA, ASTM, ISO, AOAC, USP), the limit of quantitation (LOQ) and measurement uncertainty. An LOQ above your regulatory limit makes the result useless.

3. Understand sampling and holding times

Many analyses fail before the sample reaches the lab. Request the lab's containers, preservatives and chain-of-custody forms, and ask about holding times — bacteria in water typically must be analysed within 24–30 hours.

4. Compare turnaround and rush options

Standard turnaround ranges from 24 hours (simple micro) to several weeks (stability, failure analysis). Rush fees often add 50–200%.

5. Compare total cost, not unit price

Include sampling kits, shipping (often cold-chain), sample prep, rush surcharges, report formats and re-test policies.

Red flags

  • Cannot show a current certificate or scope
  • No method references on the report
  • Prices far below market with no explanation
  • No quality-control data (blanks, spikes, duplicates) available on request
